Degree Map: when do I graduate, and what decides it?

I finished an associate degree in 2026. A degree audit lists every requirement and its status. It does not answer the question a student opens it to ask. This concept puts that answer at the top of the page and shows the reasoning underneath.

Role
Sole designer and builder
Scope
Information design, planning logic, coded prototype
When
October 2026
Status
Self-initiated concept. Invented program. Untested with students.

The prototype

The program, course codes and schedule are invented for this prototype. They are not any real college's catalog.

The design problem

A requirements list treats every remaining course as equal. They are not. Some run every term. Some run once a year. Some are locked behind a course you are taking now. One of them is usually the real constraint on when you finish, and the list gives no sign which.

In the sample program, a student with five courses left could take four of them next spring. The fifth runs only in fall. That single scheduling fact sets the graduation date, and a student who does not know it might plan a light spring for no benefit, or skip the one spring-only course and lose a term.

This framing comes from my own time as a student, not from research with other students or advisors.

Key decisions

The answer is the headline

"You can finish in Fall 2027" is the largest text on the page. The requirement list is still there, complete, in the second column. It supports the answer and no longer stands in for it.

Name the constraint

Under the date, one sentence says which course is setting it and why. If the plan changes so that a different course becomes the constraint, the sentence changes to name that one.

Plan and consequence on one screen

Ticking courses for next term updates the finish date immediately. Leave out the spring-only capstone and the headline moves to Spring 2028, with a line saying exactly what to add to get the earlier date back.

Locked courses say why

A course that cannot be taken next term stays in the list with its reason and its next opening: "Runs only in fall. The next chance is Fall 2027." Hiding it would hide the most important fact on the page.

Conditions in plain words

A course that depends on one in progress reads "Open to you if you pass SEC 230 this term". The word prerequisite does not appear. The condition is stated as what has to happen.

A flag for the thing nobody mentions

The credit total says whether the plan is a full-time or part-time load and prompts a check on financial aid. It does not pretend to know the student's aid rules. It makes sure the question gets asked before registration.

Limits and next steps

The planning logic is deliberately simple: it assumes every course is offered when the catalog says, never fills, and can be combined freely. Real schedules have time conflicts, waitlists and cancelled sections, and a real tool would have to say how confident its date is.

  • Interview eight students, half of them part-time, about how they chose last term's courses and what they found out too late.
  • Sit with two academic advisors and compare what they tell students against what the prototype says.
  • Test comprehension: after one minute with the page, can a student say when they finish and which course controls it?
  • Design for uncertainty. "Fall 2027, if SEC 250 runs" is more truthful than a bare date.
