Sanders Air Defense: identity and website

A one-person defense startup has to look credible to a program officer in the time it takes to open a link. I designed the identity and the site that carry my own company into those first ten minutes.

The reader

The people who open this site are government program staff, innovation-office scouts and potential teaming partners. They arrive from a quad chart or a submission portal, they are checking whether the company is real, and they read in a set order: what is the problem, what is your answer, how mature is it, are you eligible, how do I reach you.

That order became the page. Nothing on it is there for a general audience.

The mark

The product is called WASP, so the first logo was a wasp. The second put the wasp inside a hexagon to give it a frame. Looking at it again, I saw the problem: a hexagon is honeycomb, and honeycomb is a bee. The mark was saying the wrong insect.

Rather than fix the frame, I dropped the insect. The third direction is the product seen from above: four rotors in a square around an orange core. Each rotor is a ring that fades around its circumference, turned a quarter further than the last, so the still image reads as spinning.

The system

From the mark I built the pieces a small company uses every week: a horizontal lockup with the wordmark, print-ready business cards with bleed and safe margins, a LinkedIn banner and avatar, and the favicon. The card front holds the mark and contact details. The back holds two words and nothing else.

One production detail worth keeping: the print renderer could not draw the gradient strokes that make the rotors spin, so the card artwork rebuilds each rotor from short arc segments of decreasing opacity. Same mark, different construction for a different medium.

The website

One page, six sections, in the order a reviewer asks their questions: Problem, Solution, Demo, Readiness, About, Contact. Decisions I would defend:

  • Numbers before adjectives. The hero carries four figures a reviewer will look for anyway: system weight, technology readiness level, cost per engagement, and patent-pending claims.
  • Simulated means labelled. The animated intercept sequence and the ground-station image both say in their captions that they are simulations and not test footage. For this audience, one overclaim costs the whole page its credibility.
  • The eligibility checklist is content. Registration status and identifiers sit in the Readiness section in plain text, so a reviewer can copy them into their own system.
  • A contact button that cannot dead-end. The main call to action is an email link with a pre-filled subject. Not every machine has a mail client configured, so every email link has a Copy button beside it and a plain-text fallback.
  • Accessible basics. The mobile menu reports its open state to assistive technology, images carry real descriptions, and the layout holds at phone width.

What I'd change

Looking at the live page with fresh eyes:

  • The six application cards all use the same triangle icon. It decorates without informing. I would remove the icons or make each one mean something.
  • "~$0 per engagement" is the boldest claim on the page and has the shortest explanation. It needs one sentence on what it includes and excludes.
  • Solution and Demo are two dark sections in a row, so the page loses its rhythm halfway down.
  • There is no skip-to-content link for keyboard users.
  • I have no analytics on it. I cannot yet say which section visitors reach, and I would add privacy-respecting measurement before changing the structure.
