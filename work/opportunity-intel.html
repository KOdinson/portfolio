Defense Opportunity Intel: a feed that explains its ranking

Federal contract notices are public, and there are far too many of them. I built a tool that pulls them every morning, scores each one for fit against my company, and shows me the short list. The interface question was how to make a machine's ranking something a person can check in five seconds.

The design problem

Most tools in this market are built around search. They hand you a query box and a long list of results, and the work of deciding what matters stays with you. I wanted the opposite: no query, a short list, already sorted.

That only works if the person trusts the sort. A score of 72 means nothing by itself. So the unit of the interface became a score with its reason attached, and everything else was arranged around reading that pair quickly.

Key decisions

Score, then one sentence of why

Each card leads with the fit score in a coloured square, then the title, then a single line explaining the score in ordinary words. If the reason is wrong, I can see that it is wrong and discount the score. A number alone gives me nothing to argue with.

Checkboxes instead of a search box

Onboarding is one screen: ten capability areas, tick the ones your company works in. Each notice is tagged once against the same ten areas, so the feed is a filter and not a search. Nobody has to learn query syntax or guess keywords.

Tabs that carry their counts

Top Picks, one tab per source, and three views of past awards: largest, mid-size firms worth teaming with, and contracts ending within 18 months. Every tab shows how many items are behind it, so an empty source is visible before you click.

Tell me when the data is thin

A line under the header lists each source with a check and item count, or a cross if it failed that morning. When two government systems went down for maintenance, the dashboard said so. A short list then read as "sources are down", not "nothing is out there".

Empty states that explain

No panel is ever blank. "Nothing has scored 35+ yet. The feed refreshes every morning." tells me what the threshold is and when to look again. With no areas picked, the feed shows everything and says that it is doing so.

Always one click to the source

Every card ends with a link to the full government notice. The summary is a convenience. The notice is the authority, and the design never asks anyone to act on the summary alone.

Critique of v1

  • The thresholds disagree. A notice enters Top Picks at 35. The score square turns from grey to amber at 40. So a "top pick" can wear the colour that means low. One scale, used everywhere, fixes it.
  • The score has no scale. Nothing on the card says the number is out of 100, or what a good one looks like.
  • Deadlines are dates, not time. "Due 2026-11-14" makes the reader subtract. "Due in 9 days" does not, and lets urgent items stand out.
  • No way to disagree. When a score is wrong there is no "not relevant" control. Without that, the ranking cannot improve and the user has no voice.
  • Seven tabs in a row. They work on a desktop and will wrap badly on a phone. The three award views belong behind one tab.
  • Colour carries the tier. The number is always present, which helps, but green and amber squares need a second cue.

I am the only user. The multi-user version was built and smoke-tested locally and then parked before any outside signup. What I would do first if it restarted: sit with five people who chase contracts for small firms, show them twenty scored notices, and record where they disagree with the ranking and whether the reason line changes their mind.
