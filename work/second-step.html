Second Step: two-step sign-in people finish setting up

Two-step sign-in is one of the most effective protections an account can have, and setup is where people give up or lock themselves out later. I hold Security+, CySA+ and PenTest+. This is the setup flow I would want a non-technical relative to meet.

Role
Sole designer and builder
Scope
Flow design, content design, coded prototype
When
October 2026
Status
Self-initiated concept. Nothing in it is real. Untested with users.

The prototype

The pattern on the scan step is a decorative stand-in, not a working code, and the setup key and backup codes are generated at random in your browser.

The design problem

Security setup usually fails in one of two ways. It hides the trade-offs, so people pick the weakest option without knowing it is the weakest. Or it treats recovery as optional, so the day a phone is lost becomes the day the account is lost.

Both are content problems before they are engineering problems. The flow has four steps, and the writing in each one does most of the work.

Key decisions

Three options with their real weaknesses

Text message is described as "Easiest to start. Weaker, because a phone number can be taken over", followed by "Still far better than a password alone". The user gets the truth and is not scared out of the option they will really use.

One recommendation

The authenticator app carries a "Recommended" tag and is selected to begin with. Three equal choices is a quiz. A marked default with honest alternatives is advice.

The button repeats the choice

"Continue with text message". The label changes with the selection, so the user confirms what they picked in the act of continuing.

Errors that count for you

"Codes are 6 digits. You entered 4." A code with a letter in it gets a different message. Each one says what the system saw, which tells the user what to fix.

Backup codes cannot be skipped

The Finish button is disabled until a box is ticked: "I have saved these codes somewhere other than this phone". The reason sits beside the button: "This is the one step people regret skipping."

The warning is about the likely mistake

"Do not keep them only on the phone you just set up." A screenshot of backup codes on the phone that will be lost is the common failure, so that is the one the screen names.

The last screen is three questions with plain answers: what changes, what happens if you lose your phone, and what happens if you lose the codes too. The third answer admits a 3 to 5 day wait and explains it: "That delay is deliberate. It is what stops someone else doing it."

Limits and next steps

Forcing the backup-code step is a deliberate bit of friction, and friction in a security flow has a cost: some people will tick the box without saving anything. A checkbox proves a click and nothing more. Whether this step raises real recovery rates is exactly what would need measuring.

  • Usability sessions with people who do not work in technology, watching where they hesitate and what they do with the backup codes.
  • Follow up two weeks later: can they find their codes?
  • Compare completion with the method descriptions against a version with names only.
  • Design passkeys into the first step, since they remove the code entry entirely for people whose devices support them.
