Punchlist: maintenance requests that get fixed in one visit

In 2022 I was a maintenance specialist for a residential portfolio in Denver. Every job began with a work order somebody else had typed. This is a request form designed from the receiving end.

Role
Sole designer and builder
Scope
Flow design, content design, coded prototype
When
October 2026
Status
Self-initiated concept. Sample data. Untested with residents.

The prototype

Starting point

Most request forms are designed for the office: a category dropdown for reporting, a free-text box, a priority field. The person who has to act on it is the tech, and the tech needs different things. I started by writing down what a tech has to know before knocking on the door.

  • Which room and which fixture, so the right tools come up the stairs.
  • What it looks like, so the right part comes too. "Dripping" and "pooling on the floor" are different jobs.
  • Whether it is getting worse right now, which decides if this is today or Thursday.
  • Whether we can get in, and whether a dog is loose inside.

These are my working assumptions from doing the job, not research findings. I have not interviewed residents or other technicians for this project. The plan to validate them is at the end.

The flow

Step 1 · Where
Pick a roomEight large targets. No typing.
Step 2 · What
Pick the closest problem, then what you seeCategories are named the way a resident would say them: "Water or a leak", not "Plumbing". Symptoms appear only after a category is chosen.
Step 3 · Safety
One yes-or-no questionThe question depends on the problem. The answer sets the urgency. Categories with no safety risk skip this step.
Step 4 · Details
Photo and note, both optionalThe prompt asks for what a tech can use, with an example.
Step 5 · Access
Entry, pets, best timeThree taps.
Step 6 · Review
Read it back, sendThen a timeline that names each stage and what happens in it.

Key decisions

The form decides urgency

A priority dropdown asks the resident to rank their own problem, and everyone's problem is urgent to them. Punchlist asks a factual question instead: "Is water still running or spreading right now?" The answer sets the level, and the resident sees why.

An emergency leaves the form

If the answer signals danger, the screen says to call, in the largest type in the app, before anything else. The request can still be finished so the tech has the details, but the form never stands between a person and a phone number.

A promise on every status

Each urgency level carries a plain commitment: "We schedule this for today." It appears when the level is set, again on review, and again on the tracking screen. The resident never has to wonder what "Received" means.

Access is a required step

Permission to enter and pets are usually buried in a notes field or missed. Here they are three required taps at the end. The tracking screen then repeats the answer back as behaviour: "The tech knocks, then lets themselves in."

The button says why it is disabled

Continue stays visible on every step. When it is disabled, a line beneath it says what is missing, such as "Pick a room to continue".

Tap first, type last

A request can be filed without the keyboard opening once. The only text field is optional and comes after the structured answers have already captured the essentials.

Limits and next steps

This is a concept with one designer's assumptions in it. The response times and phone number in the prototype are placeholders, and the safety questions would need review by a property manager and against local habitability rules before real use.

What I would do next, in order:

  • Interview five residents and five maintenance techs about the last request that went badly, to test whether my four needs are the right four.
  • Collect real work orders and sort their descriptions, to check the category and symptom labels against the words people use.
  • Run task-based tests of the flow with a timer, including one emergency scenario, and watch whether people read the call instruction or tap past it.
  • Design the technician's side: the same request as it arrives on the tech's phone.
